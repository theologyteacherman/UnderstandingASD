Understanding ASD
Introduction

What is Autism
Spectrum Disorder?

ASD is a neurodevelopmental condition — it shapes how the brain develops and processes the world. It is not an illness, not caused by parenting, and not something to be cured.

Two core areas

The DSM-5 requires difficulties in both of these areas for a diagnosis. Every autistic person has them — but how they show up, and how intensely, varies enormously from person to person.

💬

Social Communication

Difficulty with back-and-forth conversation, reading social cues, eye contact, or understanding unspoken rules of interaction.

🔁

Restricted & Repetitive Behaviors

Repeating movements or phrases, strong need for routine, intense focused interests, or distress when things change unexpectedly.

Also commonly affected

These areas are not standalone diagnostic criteria, but sensory processing is explicitly recognized in the DSM-5 as an example under the restricted/repetitive behaviors criterion. Executive function difficulties are very common but are not part of the formal diagnostic criteria at all.

🌡️

Sensory Processing

Over- or under-sensitivity to sounds, lights, textures, or touch. Many autistic people find certain environments genuinely overwhelming.

🧠

Executive Function

Challenges with planning, organizing, shifting attention, or managing time — though these vary widely across individuals.

How the brain is involved

ASD is associated with differences in how certain brain areas develop and communicate with each other. Not damage — differences in wiring.

Prefrontal Cortex

Planning, decision-making, social behavior

Amygdala

Emotional processing and social responses

Cerebellum

Motor coordination and sensory prediction

Corpus Callosum

Connectivity between brain hemispheres; differences in size and structure are commonly observed in ASD

What ASD does not mean

ASD does not mean a person lacks the desire for connection or the capacity for empathy. Many autistic people experience empathy deeply — they may simply express or process it differently. Some autistic people also experience alexithymia — difficulty identifying and naming their own emotions — which can look like a lack of empathy from the outside but is something different. Many autistic people are highly intelligent. Many live full, independent lives.

A note on language. Some people prefer "autistic person" (identity-first) because they see autism as part of who they are. Others prefer "person with autism" (person-first). Neither is universally correct — follow the lead of the person you're talking with.

Identity-First

Treats autism as part of a person's identity, not something separate from them. Preferred by many autistic adults and self-advocates.

"She is autistic."

Person-First

Puts the person before the diagnosis, emphasizing that autism is one part of who they are. Preferred by some families and clinical settings.

"He is a person with autism."
History

From Asperger's to ASD:
A Brief History

For most of the 20th century, autism was seen as rare and severe. The categories used to describe it changed dramatically — and the change matters.

1943

Kanner's Autism

American psychiatrist Leo Kanner described 11 children with "early infantile autism" — profound social withdrawal, insistence on sameness, delayed or absent speech. The first formal clinical description of autism.

1944

Asperger's Work

Austrian pediatrician Hans Asperger independently described children with strong verbal ability but significant difficulty with social interaction and narrow, intense interests. His work was largely ignored in the English-speaking world for decades.

A contested legacy. It has since come to light that Asperger collaborated with the Nazi regime and referred disabled children to be killed. His legacy is now deeply contested — which is part of why many in the autism community were willing to move away from his name.
1980

DSM-III: A Formal Diagnosis

"Infantile Autism" appeared in the DSM-III, separating it from childhood schizophrenia for the first time. Autism finally had its own clinical category.

1994
DSM-IV — Multiple Categories

The Era of Separate Labels

The DSM-IV introduced several distinct diagnoses under "Pervasive Developmental Disorders." Different criteria across categories led to inconsistent diagnoses depending on which clinician you saw.

DSM-5 — Unified Diagnosis

Everything Becomes ASD

The DSM-5 collapsed all separate categories into a single diagnosis: Autism Spectrum Disorder. Instead of sorting people into boxes, clinicians now assign a support level (1–3) based on how much help a person needs. The old labels are no longer official diagnoses.

Why did the categories collapse?

Research showed that the old boundaries between Asperger's, classic autism, and PDD-NOS were unreliable. Two clinicians looking at the same person could reach different diagnoses depending on how they interpreted the criteria.

The unified ASD diagnosis was intended to improve consistency — asking "how much support does this person need?" rather than "which box do they fit?"

What about people already diagnosed with Asperger's? Their diagnosis didn't disappear — but going forward, new diagnoses use ASD. Many people who were diagnosed with Asperger's still use that term for themselves, and that is valid. Identity doesn't change just because the DSM did.

The tradeoffs

Not everyone welcomed the change. Some identified strongly with the Asperger's label and felt the unified diagnosis erased meaningful distinctions. Others worried that merging categories would make it harder to access services.

These are real concerns. The unified diagnosis is an attempt to describe a genuinely complex reality with a single term — not a perfect solution.

The Spectrum

Autism is a Spectrum —
not a line

Each person has a unique profile across multiple areas of common difficulty. DSM-5 Support Levels describe how much help is needed — but two people at the same level can look completely different.

Level
Example

Each spoke represents an area of common difficulty.

Level 1 — Requires Support

Without support: noticeable difficulties in social communication and inflexible behavior that interfere with daily functioning. "Level 1" does not mean "mildly autistic."

Level 2 — Requires Substantial Support

Without support: marked deficits in social communication and significant distress managing change or repetitive behaviors, visible to casual observers.

Level 3 — Requires Very Substantial Support

Without support: severe deficits in communication and very limited social initiation; highly restricted, repetitive behaviors cause marked interference.

Disability & Difference

Both Things
Are True

Autism is a difference in how the brain works. It is also, for many people, a disability. These two things are not in conflict.

These two truths are not opposites. They coexist.

🌿 A Difference

Autistic people think, perceive, and experience the world in genuinely different ways. Different, not broken. Many have distinctive strengths — deep focus, pattern recognition, honesty, creative thinking, intense expertise.

The neurodiversity movement argues that autism is a natural variation in human neurology — like being left-handed — not a disorder to be fixed.

⚡ A Disability

For many autistic people, the challenges are real and significant. Communication difficulties, sensory overwhelm, executive dysfunction, and social barriers can make daily life genuinely hard.

Calling it only a "difference" can erase those difficulties and make it harder to access the support and accommodations people need.

The social model of disability

The social model distinguishes between a person's impairment and the disability created by the environment around them. Same impairment, different environment — very different outcome.

The Impairment

Sensory sensitivity to sound

An autistic person is highly sensitive to noise. This is a real neurological difference.

The Environment

A loud, open-plan classroom

Designed for neurotypical students, without consideration for sensory needs.

Result: Disabling

Unable to concentrate or participate

The environment turns a difference into a barrier. Disability is partly constructed.

Change the Environment

Quiet space, noise-canceling headphones

Same person. Different environment. The disability shrinks — though the impairment remains.

The double empathy problem. Autistic sociologist and researcher Damian Milton proposed that social difficulties autistic people face reflect a mismatch between autistic and non-autistic communication styles — not a one-sided deficit. When autistic people interact with each other, social difficulties often decrease significantly.

What autistic people say

Autistic voices vary. Some strongly embrace the neurodiversity framing and resist medical language. Others — particularly parents of children with higher support needs — argue that framing severe autism as mere "difference" minimizes real suffering.

Both perspectives deserve to be heard. The tension between them is not resolved by picking a side.

"I am not less. I am not broken. I am not a puzzle to be solved. But I do need support — and naming that is not a defeat." — Composite of perspectives from autistic self-advocacy writing

The takeaway

Autism is a difference that, for many people, creates real disability. The goal is not to eliminate the difference but to reduce the barriers — through understanding, accommodation, and support — so that autistic people can live fully as themselves.

For educators and families: Accepting autism as both difference and disability means celebrating autistic strengths and taking support needs seriously. It means asking "what does this person need?" rather than "how do we make this person more typical?"
An Open Question

Should Asperger's Be
Split Off Again?

In 2013 Asperger's Syndrome was folded into ASD. Now, over a decade later, some researchers and clinicians are asking whether that was the right call — and whether it should be reversed.

The irony: The very arguments used to merge Asperger's into ASD in 2013 — inconsistent diagnosis, blurry boundaries, need for clarity — are now being used by some researchers to argue for splitting it back off. The debate has come full circle.

A quick recap

Before 2013, Asperger's Syndrome described autistic people who had no significant language delay and average or above-average intelligence. With the release of the DSM-5, Asperger's was folded into the broader autism spectrum. Most people previously diagnosed with Asperger's would correspond most closely to ASD Level 1, though support needs vary and some may fall under Level 2.

The primary reason for this change was to simplify the diagnostic process and improve consistency. The thinking was that the behaviors associated with Asperger's were not significantly different from other autism-related behaviors, and that the old distinction was often arbitrary.

Why some want to split it back off

🧬

Genetic Evidence

Recent genetic research suggests Asperger's may have a distinct neurological profile. Studies have found that the DSM-5 has high specificity for ASD broadly, but very low sensitivity toward Asperger's specifically — meaning people who would previously have been diagnosed are now slipping through without any diagnosis at all.

📋

Access to Services

Research indicates that between 25% and 50% of those who qualified for an Asperger's diagnosis under DSM-IV no longer qualify for an ASD diagnosis under DSM-5. For some, losing the diagnosis means losing access to support and accommodations they genuinely need.

🔬

Research Focus

Proponents argue that reinstating the diagnosis would lead researchers to specifically study this population — rather than having them overlooked because they don't fall within the diagnostic criteria for ASD more broadly.

🪪

Identity

Removing the Asperger's label — valued by many patients for its distinctiveness — caused real distress. Many people had built their sense of self around this diagnosis for years. Researchers have noted the potential for what they call iatrogenic harm: harm caused by the diagnostic change itself.

Why others say keep it unified

🔗 The spectrum is real

Most mainstream researchers still maintain that autism is a genuine spectrum and that Asperger's is not categorically different from ASD Level 1. Broadly, it is now agreed that what was referred to as Asperger syndrome is part of the autism spectrum.

Splitting it off risks recreating the same diagnostic inconsistencies the DSM-5 was designed to fix — two clinicians, same patient, different diagnoses.

⚠️ The name problem

Any reinstatement would face a serious ethical question. More recent research has confirmed that Hans Asperger was aware that he was sending children to their deaths at a Nazi euthanasia clinic.

Reviving his name as a clinical label — even for a genuinely distinct category — is deeply contested. A new name would likely be needed.

Where things stand now

There is no current proposal in the DSM-6 process to formally reinstate Asperger's as a separate diagnosis. But the debate is active in the research literature. Some specialists still use this profile informally — describing a subtype of ASD characterized by the absence of language delay and normal or above-average IQ — even outside the official framework.

The ICD-11, the international classification system used outside the U.S., also unified the categories in 2022 and has not signaled a reversal.

Those questions don't have clean answers.
Glossary

Words Worth
Knowing

Autism comes with a vocabulary. These terms come up constantly — in advocacy, in clinical settings, in conversation — and understanding them matters.

Concept

Neurodiversity

The idea that variation in human brain function and behavior is natural and valuable — not a flaw to be corrected. Coined by autistic Australian academic Judy Singer in the late 1990s. Autism, ADHD, dyslexia, and other conditions are framed as differences, not disorders.

Often used in advocacy contexts. Some clinicians and families prefer medical framing for access to services.
Behavior

Masking

The process of hiding or suppressing autistic traits to appear neurotypical — making eye contact, imitating social scripts, suppressing stimming. Many autistic people mask unconsciously.

Masking is exhausting and associated with burnout, anxiety, and depression. It is one reason autism is underdiagnosed in women and girls.
Behavior

Stimming

Short for "self-stimulatory behavior." Repetitive movements or sounds — rocking, hand-flapping, tapping, humming — that help regulate sensory input and emotions. Common in autistic people but present in everyone to some degree.

Stimming is not a problem to be stopped. Suppressing it often increases anxiety. It serves a real regulatory function.
Experience

Meltdown

An intense response to sensory, emotional, or cognitive overwhelm — crying, shouting, losing physical control. Not a tantrum. A meltdown is involuntary; it happens when the nervous system is pushed past its capacity.

Meltdowns are often followed by exhaustion and distress. The right response is safety and calm, not discipline.
Experience

Shutdown

The inward version of overwhelm — withdrawal, going quiet, becoming unresponsive or non-speaking. Like a meltdown, it is a nervous system response, not a choice. Often less visible and therefore less understood.

Shutdowns can be mistaken for rudeness, defiance, or depression. The person is not ignoring you — they are managing.
Experience

Autistic Burnout

A state of chronic exhaustion, reduced functioning, and increased autistic traits — often triggered by sustained masking, sensory demands, or life stress. Different from depression, though they can overlap.

Recovery can take months. Reducing demands, increasing support, and allowing unmasking are key.
Concept

Executive Function

A set of mental skills that includes planning, organization, time management, task initiation, and shifting attention. Many autistic people have significant executive function challenges — not because they lack intelligence, but because of how their brains manage competing demands.

Often misread as laziness or disorganization. Supports like written instructions, visual schedules, and extra time help significantly.
Concept

Sensory Processing

How the brain receives and interprets sensory input. Autistic people often experience hypersensitivity (overwhelmed by input) or hyposensitivity (reduced response to input) — sometimes both, in different senses.

A fire alarm, fluorescent lights, or a scratchy fabric can be genuinely painful or disabling — not dramatic. Sensory needs are real needs.
Concept

Special Interest

An area of intense, focused interest — common in autistic people. Can be almost anything: trains, ancient history, a TV show, a specific animal. The depth of engagement goes beyond typical enthusiasm.

Special interests are not a symptom to be managed — they are often a source of joy, identity, and expertise. Many autistic people build careers around them.
Concept

Neurotypical

A person whose neurological development and function is considered typical — i.e., not autistic, not ADHD, not dyslexic, etc. The term emerged from the neurodiversity movement as a neutral counterpart to "neurodivergent."

Not a judgment — just a descriptor. Neurotypical people have their own challenges. The term simply names the majority neurological pattern.
Behavior

Echolalia

Repeating words, phrases, or sounds — either immediately after hearing them (immediate echolalia) or much later, often from TV, books, or past conversations (delayed echolalia). Common in autistic people, especially children.

Echolalia is often communicative, not random. "I am the danger" might mean "I am scared." Learning to read it is worth the effort.
Concept

Double Empathy Problem

Proposed by autistic sociologist and researcher Damian Milton: the social difficulties autistic people experience are not simply a deficit on their part — they reflect a two-way mismatch between autistic and non-autistic communication styles. Both sides misread each other.

This reframes autism as a relational challenge rather than a one-sided impairment. It shifts some responsibility for communication onto neurotypical people too.

This glossary is a starting point, not a complete reference. Language in this space continues to evolve — particularly around identity-first vs. person-first terms, and the ongoing debate over "disorder" vs. "difference."
